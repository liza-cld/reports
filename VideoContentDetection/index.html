Video moderation from natural-language requirements, with a negation-aware CLIP retriever and a vision-LLM verifier

Liza Potikha · Moderation team, Cloudinary
Abstract

We introduce Video Content Detection, a new rule type that checks a video against a requirement written in plain language instead of a fixed label vocabulary. When the rule is created, an LLM extends broad terms with concrete examples where appropriate. It then restates the requirement as a simplified positive/negative prompt pair that CLIP can handle, and sets the rule's parameters for one of two rule types: existence (one frame is enough) or majority (most frames). At run time a negation-aware CLIP model (NegationCLIP, ViT-B/32) scores every frame sampled at 1 fps by its margin between the two prompts. A type-specific selector then picks at most two frames for a vision LLM to verify. Each rule makes at most two LLM calls, however long the video is. All rules on a video share a single GPU pass, and the LLM is skipped entirely when retrieval finds nothing close.

1Problem

Label-based video moderation (for example, Amazon Rekognition) can only report concepts in its fixed vocabulary. Requirements such as “must not be animated”, “no QR codes” or “free of hate symbols” are either missing from that vocabulary or depend on context that a label can't express. Sending every frame to a vision LLM would solve expressiveness, but cost and latency would grow linearly with duration: up to 600 calls for a 10-minute video, per rule. We built this rule quickly to meet customer needs, before decision models that take images as input were available. This is why a CLIP retriever comes before the LLM.

2Method

2.1 Rule creation LLM · once

The user writes the requirement R in plain language and presses Prepare rule. An LLM then performs three steps:

  1. Extends broad terms with examples, where appropriate. For a broad category it names its main kinds, so the verifier doesn't miss an instance it wouldn't file under the abstract word. Specific objects and logos get no examples. The user can edit the list, and it goes only to the verifier.
  2. Restates the requirement as a simplified positive/negative pair that CLIP can handle. CLIP's text encoder reads short, concrete captions well, but not conditions, abstract words or long lists. p+ names the simplest visual element on screen, in under 15 words, with synonyms merged and conditions that need reasoning (who, why, whether it's allowed) left to the verifier. p− is p+ with exactly one negation added. “Must be animated” and “must not be animated” get the same pair.
  3. Sets the parameters automatically. These are the rule type m, the pass direction d ∈ {found, not found}, and the image rule r, which is the requirement rewritten for a single frame. The rule type is one of two:

The added examples make the verifier's rule more detailed, while the CLIP pair stays basic: one general word, no examples. The user confirms a plain-language “What this rule checks” summary before saving, and can adjust every parameter under advanced settings.

2.2 Negation-aware frame scoring GPU · once

Frames are sampled at 1 fps, taking the first decoded frame at or after each second with its true timestamp. They are streamed at a short side of 448 px and embedded with NegationCLIP ViT-B/32, a CLIP variant fine-tuned so that its text encoder separates a caption from its negation. Each frame is scored by its contrastive margin:

st = σ( τ · [ cos(vt, e+) − cos(vt, e−) ] ),  τ ≈ 100(1)

Here τ is the model's learned logit scale. With a single negative, (1) equals a two-way softmax over {p+, p−}. Subtracting the negated prompt cancels the similarity that both captions share and keeps only the difference that matters. Frame embeddings are computed once per video and scored against every rule's prompt pair. Results are cached by hash(model, prompts) for 24 h.

2.3 Type-specific selection and gating

Existence. Frames are ranked by st, and greedy suppression drops any frame whose image embedding has cosine similarity above 0.90 to a frame already picked. The LLM is asked only if

maxt st ≥ θ = 0.05  ⇔  margin ≥ −0.029(2)

This is a high-recall gate: a frame can sit slightly closer to the negation and still be verified.

Majority. The matching share is

ρ = (1/T) · Σt 𝟙[st ≥ 0.5],  gate: ρ > π/2,  π = 50%(3)

Instead of the top frames, it verifies the frame at the cut-off rank ⌈πT⌉, the weakest frame that must still match, plus one frame about halfway up the ranking and at least 2 s away. Top-ranked frames are often unrepresentative. In a live-action ad, the title card is the frame that looks most animated.

2.4 Verification and decision LLM · ≤ 2

For each selected frame i, the vision LLM answers whether the image complies with r. This yields ai, the confidence that the content is shown. Answers are aggregated by rule type, and the direction d maps the result to a pass score:

f = maxi ai (existence) | mini ai (majority);  pass = f or 1 − f(4)

When the gate is closed, the CLIP evidence is mapped through a piecewise-linear band (θ/2, θ, 2θ) → (0, 0.5, 1). A near miss therefore gets a borderline score instead of a hard 0. A failed video cites the timestamps of the frames that broke the rule.

4Limitations

  • Sampling at 1 fps can miss content that is on screen for less than a second.
  • st is not calibrated across prompts. Existence rules rely on ranking, and majority rules on their thresholds.
  • CLIP's standard preprocessing centre-crops each frame to a square, as in OpenAI CLIP's training. On a 16:9 frame CLIP sees only the middle 56% of the width, so content near the left or right edge can score low. The vision LLM still sees the full frame. This will be covered by the switch to decision models (section 5).
  • Conditional requirements (“every rider must wear a helmet”) compile to d = found, so one compliant frame passes the video.

5Next steps

  • Replace CLIP with a decision model that takes images as input. We built this rule quickly to meet customer needs, before such models were available, so CLIP does the retrieval step. Our next step is to move retrieval to one of them.
  • Better deduplication for more efficient runs. Today, near-duplicates are removed only when picking the frames to verify. Collapsing near-identical frames, such as static shots, earlier in the pipeline will reduce the work done per video.
